Route or Distill? Investigating Lightweight Perceptual Skill Routing for Visual Quadruped Locomotion

Paper Supplementary Material
IEEE International Conference on Robotics and Automation (ICRA) 2027

(Under Review)

Abstract

Integrating visual locomotion skills enables quadrupeds to traverse diverse obstacles, but distilling specialist behaviors into a unified policy requires additional simulated rollouts and policy optimization. We ask how simply an existing specialist library can be integrated while retaining locomotion capability. We investigate lightweight terrain-based routing that composes frozen specialists through a supervised classifier trained on terrain-labeled trajectories, requiring no additional locomotion-policy optimization. Using the same specialist library, we compare routing with a DAgger-style distilled controller and ground-truth routing. Within the routing framework, we examine geometric depth features and a raw-depth convolutional network paired with instantaneous selection, exponential moving averaging, or Bayesian filtering. Experiments connect offline recognition, sequential selection, and closed-loop simulated locomotion, revealing that stronger classification accuracy and switching stability do not necessarily predict better locomotion performance. On held-out mixed-terrain courses, the best evaluated router achieves 92.6% episodic success, compared with 74.3% for the evaluated distilled checkpoint and 99.1% for ground-truth routing. These results support simple post-hoc routing as a practical integration alternative and emphasize the importance of evaluating perception and temporal selection through their closed-loop consequences.

Motivation

Quadruped robots have demonstrated remarkable advances in mobility driven by their potential to operate in unstructured environments. Quadruped robots operating in such environments must negotiate qualitatively distinct obstacles. Jumping over gaps in the terrain require controlled takeoff and landing, raised obstacles require climbing and descent, and stairs require repeated contact transitions. A useful robot must respond to the environment along a route by combining these capabilities during deployment using onboard perception. Therefore, learning specialized visual locomotion skills is only part of the problem; the system must also determine which skill to execute and when. Once specialist policies have acquired these capabilities, the remaining problem is integration; should their behaviors be consolidated into a new policy, or can they be retained and selected through a simple routing layer? Recent works have explored an increasing popular training paradigm: multi-skill policy distillation; which consolidates specialist behavior into a single, unified policy through additional online, supervised training. This produces a single deployable policy but requires an additional round of expensive simulated training and optimization. At the same time, matching specialist outputs during training does not guarantee preservation of their closed-loop performance. Moreover, an existing specialist library also enables a simpler integration strategy: learn which policy to execute while retaining the specialists themselves. Skill selection/routing avoids further locomotion-policy optimization, although it still requires training a selector and managing the transitions between skills. In this work, we ask whether lightweight post-hoc routing provides a simple and practical alternative to distillation for integrating an existing library of visual locomotion skills? We examine this question through both locomotion performance and integration effort: wherein routing retains the specialist library and adds a selector and distillation consolidates behavior into one policy. Consequently, potential savings in data collection and training must be considered alongside inference and memory costs at deployment.

We investigate lightweight terrain-based routing as a simple alternative to distilling an existing library of locomotion specialists into a unified policy. Our routed system composes frozen specialists through a supervised classifier trained on terrain-labeled simulation trajectories, requiring no additional locomotion-policy optimization. Through matched comparisons of geometric depth features and a raw-depth CNN, each paired with instantaneous selection, exponential moving averaging, or Bayesian filtering, we examine how perception and temporal processing affect routing. These choices intentionally keep the integration pipeline simple. Although more sophisticated learning frameworks could support richer skill-selection behavior, our goal is to determine whether simple classifiers and lightweight post-hoc temporal processing suffice to match or exceed the locomotion performance of the evaluated distilled controller, while measuring the integration and inference costs of both approaches. In our experiments, we connect instantaneous classification, sequential selection, and closed-loop locomotion, showing that stronger offline recognition and switching metrics do not necessarily yield better locomotion performance. Same-trajectory replay and transition-aligned analysis investigate this discrepancy through the stability, anticipation, and responsiveness of skill selection. Finally, we compare routed specialists with ground-truth routing and a distilled controller built from the same specialist library across held-out terrain sequences and difficulty levels. By jointly evaluating locomotion performance, routing behavior, deployment inference cost, and additional data-collection and training costs, we characterize when simple post-hoc routing provides a practical alternative to multi-skill distillation. Our findings suggest that such systems serves as a viable alternative to unified policy distillation for complex visual locomotion tasks and deserves further investigation.
